# FRU Company Second Brain: LLM Wiki vs RAG — Findings and Recommended Architecture

<text color="secondary" size="xs">Research and recommendations · 21 October 2026</text>

## 1. Executive conclusion

**I recommend continuing with LLM Wiki as the foundation of FRU's company second brain, but not relying on its compiled wiki as the sole source for customer Q&A.**

Your experiment has exposed an important distinction:

- **LLM Wiki is primarily a knowledge-management architecture.** It continuously builds an interconnected understanding of the company's products, operations, procedures and experience.
- **RAG is primarily a retrieval-and-grounding technique.** It locates evidence relevant to a particular question so an AI model can answer it accurately.
- **They are complementary, not mutually exclusive.** A company second brain can use LLM Wiki for knowledge compilation and a modern RAG engine for evidence retrieval.

This is consistent with Karpathy's original idea: the wiki is a persistent, evolving knowledge artifact rather than merely an alternative vector search algorithm. <Cite refs={["turn257980view0","turn257980view1"]}/>

### What your experiment actually demonstrates

Your benchmark comprises three refrigerator models, 90 questions per configuration, and four PDF preprocessing strategies.

That is the design problem I would solve next.

### The emerging practical pattern

| Company capability | Technology or method |
|---|---|
| Preserve authoritative knowledge | Original documents, versioning, provenance |
| Accumulate organizational understanding | LLM Wiki, knowledge graphs, KCS |
| Retrieve precise evidence | Hybrid RAG, metadata filters, reranking |
| Handle difficult questions | Iterative/agentic retrieval |
| Ensure safe, trustworthy answers | Evidence verification, evaluations, escalation |
| Improve with real service experience | Ticket outcomes, review and knowledge promotion |

Notice that **none of these requires abandoning LLM Wiki**. What is needed is a clear separation between knowledge accumulation and evidence-based answering.

## 3. Why your current benchmark is not yet a fair verdict

Your work is valuable, but four methodological limitations substantially affect how I would interpret it.

| Issue | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Raw RAG is vector-only | You haven't compared Wiki against a strong modern hybrid-RAG baseline. |
| Wiki and raw corpora differ | Lane D's results conflate preprocessing, content availability and retrieval. |
| Wiki cards consume top-k slots | The correct source may exist but never reach the answering LLM. |
| Thirty negative cases pass in every lane | Overall accuracy exaggerates practical Q&A performance; negative handling needs separate validation. |

Your report also identifies a particularly serious problem: the GE door-alarm question requires the **2-minute threshold**, but the relevant Wiki source text omitted it. Raw RAG had access to the original paragraph. <FileCite ref="turn0file0" line_range_start={308} line_range_end={321}/>

This is not a demonstration that vector retrieval understands the question better. It may simply be retrieving evidence that the other system no longer possesses.

### One additional finding from checking nashsu's current repository

The current implementation already documents capabilities that can help:

- A **Read Sources Only** mode.
- Search across both `wiki/` and `raw/sources/` in its built-in query pipeline.
- A local HTTP API and MCP server supporting search, source reading and graph traversal.
- A tool-using chat agent with deeper retrieval capabilities.

See the <Link url="https://github.com/nashsu/llm_wiki" title="current nashsu/llm_wiki README"/>. <Cite refs={["turn212209view4","turn212209view5","turn212209view6"]}/>

Importantly, your benchmark's custom retrieval harness is not necessarily equivalent to every retrieval mode available in the current application.

**Therefore I would improve and benchmark the integration first, rather than replacing nashsu or rewriting its knowledge compiler.**

## 4. My recommended architecture for FRU

I would build a **dual-layer knowledge system**:

1. A compiled, continuously maintained LLM Wiki for company intelligence.
2. A lossless, source-grounded retrieval system for precise product and service Q&A.

Both should be derived from the same authoritative sources, sharing document IDs, model IDs and provenance.

### The three essential knowledge layers

| Layer | Contents | Responsibility |
|---|---|---|
| **Source of truth** | Original PDF, faithful Markdown, specifications, revisions | Preserve exactly what documents state |
| **Compiled intelligence** | LLM Wiki entity pages, topic shards, relationships, synthesized learnings | Organize and connect knowledge |
| **Evidence access** | Lexical and vector indexes, reranker, source locator | Recover precise information for answers |

For example, the Wiki can establish that a particular Samsung refrigerator has a filter replacement process, relationships to compatible parts, common symptoms and relevant service experiences.

But when a customer asks which button to press for exactly three seconds, the answer should be verified against the applicable original instructions—not trusted merely because a Wiki summary mentions filter replacement.

### An important additional layer: structured business information

Some company knowledge should remain in structured systems rather than become free-form Markdown.

Examples include SKU aliases, serial-number applicability, part compatibility, warranty eligibility, current stock and ticket status.

The Wiki can describe and connect those facts, but **current operational values should be obtained from their authoritative databases or APIs**. This prevents an outdated compiled page from answering a warranty or stock question incorrectly.

## 5. How FRU's customer Q&A should operate

I recommend progressive retrieval, with the cost and complexity of each search determined by the evidence requirements.

<box border radius="xl" padding={3} gap={2}>
  <text weight="medium" size="sm">Example: Customer asks about a refrigerator alarm</text>
  <box background="surface-secondary" radius="lg" padding={3}>
    <text size="sm">"My GE GNE27JYMFS fridge door is open. How long before the alarm sounds, and how do I stop it?"</text>
  </box>
  <list marker="number" connector="solid" gap={2}>
    <list-item>
      **Identify the product and user type**

      Extract model `GNE27JYMFS`, identify a customer-safe operating question, and restrict product evidence to the applicable manual/version.
    </list-item>
    <list-item>
      **Perform cheap retrieval first**

      Search exact model and terms such as "door alarm", "beep" and "open". Use lexical and vector search with metadata filtering.
    </list-item>
    <list-item>
      **Check evidence completeness**

      The retrieved passages must support both the delay and the action that stops the alarm. Finding only a general "door open" symptom is insufficient.
    </list-item>
    <list-item>
      **Escalate retrieval if necessary**

      Expand into the controls section, consult the Wiki's topic relationships, then open the original relevant passage. Avoid repeatedly returning generic concept cards.
    </list-item>
    <list-item>
      **Answer with citation or abstain**

      Report the precise documented delay and stopping action, citing the manual and page. If the required detail cannot be established, say so rather than guess.
    </list-item>
  </list>
</box>

The essential innovation is the **evidence-completeness check**.

You raised this issue in your earlier Q&A evaluation work: how can the system know whether its answer is good enough before committing?

I would define that operationally as:

- All required answer components have supporting evidence.
- The evidence applies to the correct product and revision.
- No material contradiction or missing prerequisite remains.
- The response contains no unsupported factual or procedural claims.

A high vector similarity score, attractive Wiki page or self-reported LLM confidence cannot substitute for these checks.

There is no infallible, universal "good enough" score. But you can measure component coverage and use the result to trigger further retrieval. AWS's current RAG evaluation framework explicitly distinguishes context coverage, answer correctness, faithfulness and citation quality. <Cite refs={["turn444279search0","turn180432search3"]}/>

## 6. The experiment I would run next

I would **stop experimenting with entirely new PDF formats for the moment**. You have already learned enough to identify an improved architecture.

The next step is to isolate which component delivers incremental value.

### Proposed Pass 3 — controlled architecture comparison

| Configuration | Source preparation | Retrieval |
|---|---|---|
| A — Existing baseline | Faithful raw manual extracts | Vector-only |
| B — Strong RAG baseline | Same raw extracts | Hybrid lexical + vector + reranker |
| C — Enriched RAG | Raw extracts + Prompt C topic shards | Same hybrid + reranker |
| D — Existing Wiki | Prompt C + nashsu compiled Wiki | Wiki hybrid |
| E — Proposed FRU system | Raw + Prompt C + Wiki | Wiki-guided retrieval, source verification, iterative fallback |

**Configuration B is especially important.** You have not yet established how much improvement is possible simply by fixing RAG retrieval without the LLM Wiki compilation step.

Configuration C then isolates the incremental contribution of your preprocessing work. E tests whether compilation and more advanced retrieval genuinely complement each other.

For all five configurations, control the source-document versions, answering model, evaluation questions and judge settings. Record the differing evidence available to each arm so that extraction losses aren't mistaken for retrieval failures.

### Evaluation improvements

Keep the existing 90 questions as a development/regression suite, but introduce a genuinely held-out test set drawn from additional models and, ideally, real FRU customer inquiries.

The next test should distinguish:

| Metric | What it reveals |
|---|---|
| Source fact coverage | Did preprocessing preserve the required facts? |
| Complete evidence recall@k | Did retrieval find **all** evidence necessary to answer? |
| Answer correctness | Did the LLM produce the correct complete answer? |
| Wrong-SKU rate | Did it mix information across models? |
| Citation validity | Do citations actually support each claim? |
| Negative-case handling | Can it refuse unsupported questions without refusing answerable ones? |
| End-to-end latency and cost | Is the pipeline practical to deploy? |

For hard questions, explicitly label the multiple independent evidence passages required. A case should not receive full evidence-recall credit just because one out of three necessary passages was retrieved.

Keep the evaluator blinded to expected pass/fail distributions and independently audit disputed results. I would also classify failures by extraction, missing evidence, ranking, answer generation and judging, rather than record only a single pass/fail.

Microsoft's current RAG evaluator documentation supports this separation between retrieval-process and final-answer evaluation. <Cite ref="turn180432search3"/>

## 7. How this becomes a genuine company second brain

The most important expansion beyond PDFs will be learning from **actual customer-service outcomes**.

Consider the following hypothetical FRU case:

A customer reports poor cooling on model X. The assistant finds an approved diagnostic procedure and recommends checking a particular airflow obstruction. A technician follows it, fixes the issue, and records the outcome.

The system should preserve the case but distinguish three things:

| Information | Knowledge status |
|---|---|
| OEM repair instructions | Authoritative documented procedure |
| AI-recommended repair based on a similar model | Hypothesis; not verified for this model |
| Technician-confirmed successful repair | Observed outcome in a specific case |

One successful outcome is not automatic proof that the recommendation works universally. It becomes a candidate for reusable knowledge, subject to model applicability, causal evidence and review.

This is where LLM Wiki has a substantial architectural advantage over a simple document-search system: it can maintain the accumulated relationships and lessons, while the underlying records and provenance remain available.

The KCS approach specifically separates reusable knowledge articles from customer-specific incident records and advocates improving knowledge in the normal service workflow. <Cite refs={["turn444279search7","turn444279search15"]}/>

For FRU, that means implementing:

**Ticket → observed resolution → proposed knowledge update → validation → approved Wiki/service article.**

It should not be:

**Ticket closed → AI automatically assumes recommendation was correct → publishes as verified fact.**

This also creates a practical division of access. Customers see approved operating guidance, support agents can see internal troubleshooting, and authorized technicians can access restricted repair procedures. Unsafe electrical or refrigerant servicing instructions should never be exposed indiscriminately.

Your work has not undermined the LLM Wiki direction. It has clarified what that direction should mean for a real business.

Your **Chief of Staff (COS) analogy is very good**, and I think it exposes a more fundamental question than the one we have been testing:

**If an LLM Wiki cannot reliably outperform conventional RAG in information retrieval, what justifies the additional complexity, cost and potential inaccuracies of building a company second brain?**

My answer is:

**A second brain is valuable if it makes the company collectively more knowledgeable and more effective at using that knowledge—not merely if it makes document search better.**

But there is an important qualification: if FRU's primary objective is simply to answer customer questions from refrigerator manuals, I would not recommend building an LLM Wiki just for that. A well-engineered RAG system is likely a more economical starting point.

## 1. Your Chief of Staff analogy is largely correct

| Human Chief of Staff | LLM Wiki equivalent |
|---|---|
| Reads reports, manuals, emails and meeting notes | Ingests company documents and operational records |
| Builds a mental understanding of the company | Compiles entity pages, topic summaries and relationships |
| Connects facts across different departments | Maintains cross-document knowledge links |
| Recalls relevant information when management asks | Retrieves related Wiki knowledge |
| Combines multiple pieces into a coherent briefing | Synthesizes answers using an LLM |
| Sometimes misremembers or overlooks details | Can omit, distort or hallucinate information |

However, consider what a **good** Chief of Staff actually does.

When the CEO asks, "What was our revenue last quarter?", the COS shouldn't rely exclusively on a remembered summary. They should check the authoritative financial report.

When asked, "Why are warranty expenses rising, and what should we do?", the COS can use their accumulated understanding to connect information from sales, repairs, customers, products and finance—but should still verify the important facts.

That distinction is fundamental:

**The COS's memory is for understanding and connecting information. The company's records are for establishing factual truth.**

Your LLM Wiki should operate according to the same principle.

## 2. Why management might be disappointed

I agree with your concern. There is a mismatch between what management expects from a computer system and what an LLM-based knowledge system actually does.

| Management's expectation | Technical reality |
|---|---|
| "A computer should find the correct information." | Retrieval can miss relevant information. |
| "The system has read all our documents." | Summarization may have discarded important details. |
| "It knows our company's products." | It may confuse similar products or versions. |
| "It sounds confident, so it must know." | Fluent answers do not establish factual correctness. |
| "It should improve as we add documents." | More data can also create contradictions and retrieval noise. |

**We should not expect management to understand or accept these limitations as excuses.**

Instead, the system needs an explicit reliability contract.

I would make it distinguish three types of answer:

## 3. So where is the real business value?

I see four potential advantages, but they need to be demonstrated rather than assumed.

| Capability | Conventional RAG | Compiled Second Brain |
|---|---|---|
| Find a specific fact in a manual | Strong with good indexing | No inherent advantage |
| Synthesize knowledge across many sources | Possible at query time | Can reuse precompiled synthesis |
| Maintain a continuously evolving map of company knowledge | Requires additional machinery | Central design objective |
| Detect contradictions, gaps and relationships proactively | Requires additional workflows | Natural extension of Wiki maintenance |
| Preserve discoveries and lessons from previous investigations | Requires a persistent learning layer | Central design objective |

Importantly, **a sufficiently advanced RAG system can implement every one of these capabilities too**.

LLM Wiki is not a magic algorithm with abilities RAG cannot reproduce. It is a different architectural choice: invest work *when information arrives*, so the resulting knowledge becomes reusable rather than reconstructing everything on demand.

That is Karpathy's explicit rationale. His original design describes immutable raw sources, a continuously maintained synthesis layer, and periodic checks for contradictions and missing knowledge. <Cite refs={["turn959295view0","turn716056view0","turn716056view1"]}/>

### The stronger FRU business example

Suppose management asks:

*"Why are our Samsung refrigerator warranty expenses increasing, which products are driving the problem, and what can we do about it?"*

A simple manual-search system won't answer that question. Even searching thousands of manuals would be insufficient.

The relevant knowledge may exist across service tickets, model histories, parts replacements, supplier notices, warranty costs and previous management discussions.

An effective second brain could maintain relationships among all those sources, identify emerging patterns, surface previously recorded hypotheses and assemble an evidence-backed briefing.

This is closer to what an excellent COS does.

However, an agent with access to the same records and an appropriate RAG/search system could also do this. **The Wiki's potential advantage is reusing previously established relationships and syntheses, not some fundamentally superior reasoning capability.**

The business value must come from reducing repeated investigation, preserving institutional knowledge and improving decisions.

This aligns with the established Knowledge-Centered Service methodology, which emphasizes capturing, reusing and improving knowledge during actual service work. <Cite ref="turn959295search0"/>

| Proposition | FRU success criterion |
|---|---|

## My conclusion

I think your COS analogy reveals both the promise and the risk of LLM Wiki.

**A Chief of Staff is not valuable because their memory is a better search engine than the company's document repository. They're valuable because they maintain context, connect information, remember previous decisions, identify problems and help management act.**

But a competent COS also knows when to consult the original records.

That's the version of LLM Wiki I would build for FRU.

And one particularly important point: **Karpathy's original design already keeps immutable raw sources as the source of truth.** The separation between accumulated understanding and authoritative evidence is not a compromise that weakens LLM Wiki; it is consistent with the original architecture. <Cite ref="turn716056view0"/>

The business case for the second brain is therefore not *better search than RAG*. It is **compounding organizational knowledge that can be reliably verified and put to work**.

# FRU Second Brain — 11 Questions, Practical Answers

My updated position is: **Keep LLM Wiki for organizational knowledge accumulation, use modern RAG for precise evidence retrieval, and design the two to work together.** But importantly, we should test whether LLM Wiki produces measurable benefits beyond a well-engineered agentic RAG system.

## Part 1 — Is LLM Wiki's underlying rationale valid?

### 1–2. Is precompiled synthesis really more efficient than live RAG?

**Potentially, but not necessarily. Your reasoning is correct.**

Consider a management question requiring information from five documents.

| | LLM Wiki | Agentic RAG |
|---|---|---|
| When synthesis happens | Primarily during ingestion | Primarily when queried |
| Query-time work | Retrieve existing synthesis, verify | Retrieve documents, synthesize |
| Repeated questions | May reuse previous work | May repeat work, unless cached |
| New/unexpected questions | May need fresh retrieval anyway | Can investigate directly |
| Weakness | Precompiled knowledge can be incomplete or incorrect | Repeated search and reasoning costs |

If live RAG is sufficiently fast, **there may be no user-visible speed advantage from precompilation**.

The useful comparison is total cost:

\[
C_{\text{Wiki}}=C_{\text{compile+maintain}}+N C_{\text{query}}
\]

\[
C_{\text{RAG}}=C_{\text{index}}+N C_{\text{query}}
\]

where \(N\) is the number of questions.

A Wiki only wins economically if its savings from repeated investigation justify compilation and maintenance costs. Faster response time is a separate consideration.

**One further complication:** RAG can cache verified answers and reusable analyses. Thus, persistent synthesis is not exclusive to LLM Wiki.

### 3. How does a continuously maintained knowledge map create concrete business benefits?

I would measure four capabilities rather than treating the existence of an attractive Wiki as proof of value.

| Wiki capability | Concrete FRU example | Business KPI |
|---|---|---|
| Cross-source synthesis | Connect warranty costs, repair tickets and supplier changes | Analyst-hours per investigation |
| Relationships | Identify recurring faults across product models or component families | Time to identify emerging problems |
| Contradiction detection | Flag conflicting service procedures or superseded bulletins | Conflicts discovered before incorrect advice |
| Institutional memory | Reuse an earlier investigation when the same issue recurs | Repeat-investigation effort, onboarding time |

These are **potential benefits, not yet demonstrated results**.

A particularly useful experiment would be asking the same 20 cross-functional questions of a Wiki-based assistant and a capable agentic RAG assistant, measuring answer quality, elapsed time and cost.

### 4. Could "study first, answer later" be a misleading human analogy?

**Yes. This is a legitimate challenge to the core LLM Wiki rationale.**

Humans study because their working memory and retrieval abilities are limited. AI systems can search thousands of documents, revisit them and synthesize information on demand.

An AI system does not necessarily benefit from pre-learning in the same way a human does.

Karpathy's central insight isn't that an LLM develops better memory like a human. It is that information can become a **persistent, maintained and reusable synthesis**, with relationships and contradictions integrated as new sources arrive. <Cite ref="turn202500view1"/>

That is an engineering trade-off, not an automatic superiority over RAG.

I would treat the following as our hypothesis:

> Precompiled organizational knowledge becomes valuable when maintaining and reusing cross-source insights is cheaper or more effective than repeatedly rediscovering them.

**We should test this hypothesis, rather than assume it is true.**

### 5. How do we prevent inaccuracy from contaminating the second brain?

This is probably the most important concern you raised.

I would distinguish two kinds of imperfection:

- **Incomplete Wiki:** It doesn't know something. The assistant can search original records or acknowledge missing information.
- **Incorrect Wiki:** It confidently knows something false. This can contaminate future summaries, relationships and answers.

The second is much more dangerous, particularly if one inferred fact becomes the supposed evidence for other inferred facts.

**A dual-layer architecture is necessary, but not sufficient.** We also need claim-level provenance and controls.

I recommend storing every important compiled claim with:

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Claim | Door alarm sounds after 2 minutes |
| Model | `GNE27JYMFS` |
| Evidence | Source document ID, revision, page and exact passage |
| Status | Grounded / Inferred / Contradicted / Unverified |
| Validation | Automated check and, where required, human approval |

**Practical safeguards:**

1. Never treat an unsupported Wiki summary as authoritative evidence.
2. Verify important numbers, error codes, part numbers and procedures against original passages.
3. Keep hypotheses separate from grounded facts.
4. Recompile dependent knowledge when the source changes.
5. Prevent summaries from recursively citing other summaries without traceable original evidence.

The original Karpathy architecture explicitly keeps raw sources immutable and separate from the LLM-maintained Wiki. Our additional controls would make that approach safer for a business. <Cite ref="turn302064view5"/>

These mechanisms can greatly reduce contamination, but cannot guarantee zero inaccuracies. Source documents themselves can be wrong or obsolete, and automated verification can miss errors.

## Part 2 — How to improve retrieval and learn from tickets

### 6. What exactly is Agentic RAG? Isn't repeated search deterministic?

**Your objection is correct if the agent simply repeats the same query.**

Same query + same documents + same deterministic retrieval algorithm = same results.

But useful agentic retrieval does something different.

Consider the question:

*"What steps must a technician follow to diagnose a Samsung refrigerator that isn't cooling, and what temperatures should be checked?"*

The agent doesn't know the correct answer in advance. It uses the question to identify **required information**, then checks whether its evidence contains that information.

This is the approach described in current AWS agentic retrieval documentation: decompose questions, retrieve iteratively, assess sufficiency and open source documents when necessary. <Cite ref="turn196126view4"/>

But your skepticism identifies a real limitation: **iterative retrieval cannot recover a fact that is absent from the entire indexed corpus**, nor guarantee that an LLM has recognized every missing detail.

#### Simplest enhancement to your RAG implementation

I would begin with a bounded, deterministic workflow—not a completely autonomous agent.

| Stage | Implementation |
|---|---|
| 1 | Extract SKU and question intent |
| 2 | Hybrid search; retrieve 20–30 candidates |
| 3 | Rerank and inspect the strongest evidence |
| 4 | Check whether required answer components are supported |
| 5 | If incomplete, generate targeted subqueries and expand original sections |
| 6 | Stop after a fixed budget; answer or abstain |

**Start with one retrieval pass plus at most one targeted expansion.** Make the number of additional searches configurable and measure whether they actually help.

## Part 3 — Preprocessing architecture

### 9. Should Prompt B and Prompt C v4 both become read-only raw sources?

**No—not as two equally authoritative copies of the same manual.**

I would distinguish three representations:

| Representation | Purpose | Authoritative? |
|---|---|---|
| Original PDF | Ultimate documentary record | Yes, subject to revision |
| Faithful, verified PDF-to-Markdown extraction | Searchable equivalent of the original | Yes, within verified coverage |
| Prompt C v4 topic-organized knowledge | Improved retrieval, explanation, compilation | Derived, not independently authoritative |

Your Lane B page-derived Markdown lost some facts that remained in consolidated extracts. Therefore, I would not automatically make Lane B the canonical text representation.

Instead, choose or construct **one verified canonical Markdown extract per PDF**. Preserve page boundaries, tables, figures and original source mapping. Keep the PDF itself.

Store Prompt C v4 as a derived representation with links back to the canonical source.

### 10. Where exactly does Anthropic's Contextual Retrieval fit?

It fits **between chunk creation and index construction**.

It does not require changing the original manual or replacing Prompt C.

Example:

<box background="surface-secondary" radius="lg" padding={3} gap={2}>

  <text color="secondary" weight="medium" size="xs">Indexed text</text>

Both BM25 and embeddings index the contextualized version. The answering LLM still receives the exact passage and its citation.

Anthropic's published approach uses an LLM to generate roughly 50–100 tokens of context per chunk, based on the larger document, before embedding and BM25 indexing. <Cite ref="turn196126view0"/>

It also evaluates reranking *after* initial retrieval, so these are two separate enhancements. <Cite ref="turn196126view1"/>

For FRU, I would implement this incrementally:

- First test a deterministic prefix: manufacturer, SKU, manual title, version, section and page.
- Then test an LLM-generated chunk-specific description.
- Keep the original chunk unchanged and prohibit the description from introducing unsupported facts.

This lets you measure whether expensive LLM-generated context adds value over relatively cheap structural metadata.

# FRU Second Brain — Verification, Iterative Retrieval and Search Architecture

There are **two different engineering problems** we need to solve:

1. **Knowledge integrity:** How do we prevent nashsu LLM Wiki from accumulating incorrect knowledge during compilation?
2. **Answer reliability:** How do we retrieve sufficiently complete evidence for each customer question?

They require different checks. A single LLM judge cannot reliably solve both.

## 1. How do we verify LLM Wiki summaries?

**Recommendation: Verify individual factual claims against original evidence, not entire summaries using a voting panel.**

A panel of different LLMs may catch some mistakes, but several models can agree on the same incorrect interpretation. Agreement is not evidence.

### Proposed verification pipeline

  <CodeBlock language="mermaid" model_previewable>
flowchart TD

 F -->|"Supported"| G["Publish as grounded knowledge"]
 F -->|"Contradicted"| H["Block + correction review"]
 F -->|"Insufficient evidence"| I["Mark unverified / review"]
 G --> J["Wiki and knowledge graph"]
 classDef source fill:#dbeafe,stroke:#2563eb,color:#17365d
 classDef pass fill:#d1fae5,stroke:#059669,color:#064e3b
 classDef issue fill:#fef3c7,stroke:#d97706,color:#78350f
 class A,B,C,D,E source
 class G,J pass
 class H,I issue
  </CodeBlock>
</box>

For example, suppose nashsu creates this Wiki statement:

> The GE GNE27JYMFS door alarm sounds after two minutes when enabled.

The verifier should:

1. Extract the atomic claim: `door_alarm_delay = 2 minutes`, with model and conditions.
2. Retrieve the corresponding **original manual passage**.
3. Compare the claim with that passage, including conditions and units.
4. Return `SUPPORTED`, `CONTRADICTED` or `INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE`, with an exact citation.
5. Publish it as grounded knowledge only if verification passes.

Use deterministic checks where possible (model number, numeric values, units, source path); use a second LLM for semantic comparison and interpretation.

### Do we need a judge panel?

| Verification method | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Same LLM reviews its own summary | Weak independence |
| Different LLM checks claim against original evidence | **Yes — default** |
| Three LLM judges voting | Optional for difficult/high-risk cases |
| Deterministic numeric, SKU and citation checks | **Yes — essential** |
| Human approval | For unresolved or safety-critical knowledge |

The distinction is important: **the judge must read the evidence, not merely decide whether a summary sounds correct.**

To keep this efficient, I would verify each *new or modified factual claim* during ingestion, cache the result by claim and source-version hash, and recheck only affected claims after source changes. Human review is reserved for riskier or disputed claims.

Even with this approach, we cannot guarantee that every meaningful fact in the original document appears in the Wiki. **Accuracy and completeness need separate tests.** Critical specifications, warnings and troubleshooting steps should receive source-coverage checks in addition to claim verification.

nashsu already generates source references and has an asynchronous review mechanism, but that is not equivalent to automatic claim-level verification and publication gating. The latter would be an FRU-specific enhancement. <Cite ref="turn415201view5"/>

## 2. How does iterative targeted retrieval work, specifically?

Let's use our earlier Samsung example.

**Question:** "My Samsung RF28K9070SG isn't cooling properly. What should I check, and what freezer temperature range can I select?"

### First retrieval

<box border radius="lg" padding={3} gap={2}>
  <text weight="medium" size="sm">Step 1 — Identify required answer components</text>
  <table>
    <table-row><table-cell>Cooling troubleshooting checks</table-cell><table-cell><badge color="success">Found</badge></table-cell></table-row>
    <table-row><table-cell>Freezer selectable temperature range</table-cell><table-cell><badge color="warning">Missing</badge></table-cell></table-row>
  </table>
  <text color="secondary" size="xs">Illustrative retrieval state, not an actual new benchmark run.</text>
</box>

The system doesn't know the correct temperature range, but it **knows the user requested one**.

A small LLM call or structured planner identifies the required answer components and assesses whether the retrieved passages explicitly support each component.

### Targeted expansion

Instead of repeating the original search, it creates a *different* search:

<box background="surface-secondary" padding={3} radius="lg" gap={2}>
  <text color="secondary" size="xs">Original search</text>
  <text size="sm">`RF28K9070SG not cooling diagnosis troubleshooting`</text>
  <divider color="subtle"/>
  <text color="secondary" size="xs">Second, targeted search</text>
  <text size="sm">`RF28K9070SG freezer temperature setting range controls`</text>
</box>

The second search can retrieve the freezer-controls section that the first search overlooked.

Your existing benchmark identifies a freezer setting range of **5°F to −8°F** in its gold answer for this model. The search must locate and verify that range in the source before responding.

If a candidate chunk is cut off, the system opens the neighboring chunks or full section rather than just increasing the ranking score.

### Simplified implementation

```python
query = parse_question(question)
evidence = hybrid_search(query, model_id, top_k=20)
evidence = rerank(question, evidence)[:5]

coverage = check_required_evidence(question, evidence)

if not coverage.complete:
    subquery = generate_query_for_missing(coverage)
    additional = hybrid_search(subquery, model_id, top_k=20)
    evidence = rerank(question, evidence + additional)[:8]

return answer_or_abstain(question, evidence)
```

The second search still may fail. Therefore, the final answer requires an evidence check.

**The important distinction:** We are not repeating the same deterministic search. We are searching for specifically missing information, possibly in a different document section.

I would initially limit this to one additional round and measure how often it rescues an otherwise failed answer.

## 3. What exactly is semantic reranking? Will it help?

**Probably, but it must be tested.**

BM25 and vector search retrieve candidate chunks efficiently. Reranking uses a more computationally expensive model to inspect the **question and each candidate together** and determine which passage is most relevant.

<box gap={2}>
  <text weight="medium" size="sm">FRU hybrid retrieval with reranking</text>
  <CodeBlock language="mermaid" model_previewable>
flowchart TD
 A["Customer question + SKU"] --> B["SKU metadata filter"]
 B --> C["BM25 keyword search"]
 B --> D["Vector similarity search"]
 C --> E["RRF: merge and deduplicate"]
 D --> E
 E --> F["Top 20–30 candidate passages"]
 F --> G["Semantic reranker<br/>Evaluate question + passage"]
 G --> H["Top 5–8 passages"]
 H --> I["Evidence verification + answer"]
 classDef retrieval fill:#dbeafe,stroke:#2563eb,color:#17365d
 classDef rank fill:#ede9fe,stroke:#7c3aed,color:#40306c
 classDef output fill:#d1fae5,stroke:#059669,color:#064e3b
 class A,B,C,D,E,F retrieval
 class G,H rank
 class I output
  </CodeBlock>
</box>

For example, a query mentioning *temperature* may initially rank a repair diagnostic temperature sensor section above the user-controls section that actually contains the requested selectable range. A semantic reranker can promote the more directly answer-bearing passage.

| Method | What it evaluates |
|---|---|
| BM25 | Exact words and their statistical importance |
| Vector similarity | Semantic resemblance |
| RRF | Combines ranking positions |
| **Semantic reranker** | How directly a candidate passage addresses the specific question |

It is not necessarily a general-purpose LLM. You can use a specialized reranking model, such as a cross-encoder, or a hosted reranking API.

Microsoft documents reranking as a separate step after BM25/RRF, applied to a limited candidate set. <Cite refs={["turn924890search0","turn924890search1"]}/>

**Limitation:** Reranking cannot discover a missing passage outside the retrieved candidates. It improves precision within the candidate set; it does not guarantee complete evidence recall.

For FRU, I recommend a controlled A/B test of hybrid search with and without reranking, measuring answerable accuracy, complete-evidence recall and added latency.

**Normally, no.** A typical cross-encoder reranker evaluates the question and candidate passage directly, producing its own relevance score.

| Chunk | BM25 rank | Vector rank | Semantic reranker score* |
|---|---:|---:|---:|

RRF uses the BM25/vector **rank positions** to select candidates. The reranker then independently evaluates those candidates.

It is possible to build a custom learning-to-rank system that also uses BM25, similarity scores and other features, but that is **not necessary for our initial implementation**.

## 1. Why can a cross-encoder reranker outperform vector search and RRF?

The key difference is **how deeply the model examines the relationship between the question and each passage**.

| Method | How it ranks | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| **Vector search (bi-encoder)** | Encodes question and passage independently, then compares their vectors | Fine-grained relationships can be lost in the embeddings |
| **BM25** | Matches terms using their frequency and importance | Doesn't deeply understand meaning |
| **RRF** | Combines existing BM25/vector rank positions | Doesn't examine the text again |
| **Cross-encoder reranker** | Processes the question and passage **jointly**, allowing token-level interactions | More computationally expensive |

You're close, but there's one important misconception: **BERT doesn't work like GPT's next-word prediction, and a BERT-based reranker doesn't rank passages by how likely their next words are.**

## 1. BERT vs GPT — the fundamental difference

Both use the Transformer architecture, but are trained differently.

| | BERT | GPT |
|---|---|---|
| Architecture | Transformer encoder | Transformer decoder |
| Original training task | Predict **masked words** using surrounding context | Predict **next token** using preceding context |
| Strength | Understanding relationships in text | Generating text |
| For reranking | Produces a relevance score | Can also judge relevance, but generally with more overhead |

For example:

- **GPT:** "The freezer temperature is ..." → predict what comes next.
- **BERT:** "The freezer [MASK] is adjustable." → use words on **both sides** to determine the missing word.

However, a reranker uses BERT differently from its original training task.

## 2. How does BERT reranking actually work?

Imagine we have the question:

**"What is the selectable freezer temperature range?"**

And two retrieved chunks from the refrigerator manual.

  <box background="surface-secondary" radius="lg" padding={3} gap={1}>

    "The freezer temperature sensor is used to monitor cooling performance..."
  </box>
  <box background="surface-secondary" radius="lg" padding={3} gap={1}>
    <text weight="medium" size="xs" color="secondary">Candidate B — Temperature controls</text>
    "The freezer temperature can be adjusted from 5°F to −8°F..."
  </box>
  <text color="secondary" size="xs">Illustrative passages.</text>
</box>

A BERT-based cross-encoder is typically **fine-tuned specifically to predict relevance**, using training examples of questions and passages.

The model can learn that:

- "Selectable range" relates closely to "can be adjusted from X to Y."
- "Temperature sensor" mentions the right general subject but doesn't answer the actual question.

Thus Candidate B can receive a higher relevance score even if Candidate A had ranked higher in the original vector search.

**It is not predicting the next word. It is predicting how relevant the passage is to the question.**

## 3. So where does the knowledge of the manuals come in?

This is the critical part.

**The reranker generally has not been trained on your refrigerator manuals.**

It has two sources of information:

| Source | What it contributes |
|---|---|
| Pretraining and relevance fine-tuning | General language understanding and learned patterns of what makes a passage relevant |
| **Actual manual chunk supplied at query time** | Specific facts about the refrigerator |

Think of the reranker as an experienced librarian.

The librarian doesn't need to memorize refrigerator manuals. They understand your question well enough to decide which retrieved passage is most likely to answer it.

The actual refrigerator knowledge comes from **the text of the retrieved passages**, not necessarily from the model's internal knowledge.

This also explains an important limitation:

If none of the candidate chunks contains the correct temperature range, **even the best reranker cannot retrieve it from nowhere**. It can only rank the candidates it receives.

---

### The key distinction to remember

- **Vector search:** "Which passages have similar meanings to this question?"
- **RRF:** "Which passages rank well across different search methods?"
- **Cross-encoder:** "After reading this question and this passage together, how directly does this passage address the question?"
- **Answer LLM:** "Using the retrieved evidence, what is the answer?"

For FRU, the cross-encoder acts as a more careful **relevance judge**, not a refrigerator expert and not a factual accuracy verifier.

That's why we need the additional `EvidenceVerifier`: **a highly relevant passage is not necessarily sufficient or factually correct evidence for the entire answer.**

# High-quality PDF → Canonical Markdown: Recommended FRU Pipeline

**My recommendation, based on current tooling as of October 2026: use a hybrid extraction pipeline combining Docling, PyMuPDF, selective vision-LLM calls and automated quality checks.**

Don't ask one LLM to read an entire PDF and rewrite it as Markdown. And don't depend exclusively on conventional text extraction.

For complex refrigerator manuals, the goal is **faithful reconstruction**, not summarization: preserve every specification, procedure, warning, troubleshooting table and important diagram, with traceability to the original PDF pages.

## 1. What I would choose

| Tool | Role | Why |
|---|---|---|
| <Entity category="software" value="Docling" disambig="IBM open-source document parsing toolkit"/> | Primary layout-aware PDF → Markdown extraction | Handles headings, tables, OCR, reading order and figures |
| <Entity category="software" value="PyMuPDF" disambig="PDF processing and extraction library"/> | Independent text extraction, page inspection, rendering and QA | Fast, deterministic access to the original PDF |
| Vision-capable LLM API | Repair or verify problematic regions | Understands visually complex tables, diagrams and corrupted text |
| Python QA logic | Detect missing content and suspicious extraction | Prevents silent losses before publishing |

These are complementary rather than competing tools.

Recent independent parser comparisons show substantial document-dependent variation: Docling performs well on tables, while Marker and MinerU can outperform it on particular scans or layouts. No single parser wins consistently. <Cite refs={["turn391299search2","turn391299search6"]}/>

An additional candidate worth benchmarking is <Link url="https://opendataloader.org/docs/hybrid-mode" title="OpenDataLoader Hybrid"/>. Its published benchmarks report strong table and reading-order performance using selective complex-page routing, though the benchmark is maintained by its own project and should be validated on FRU's manuals. <Cite refs={["turn391299search1","turn440836search2"]}/>

import os
import re
import json
import base64
import hashlib
from pathlib import Path

import fitz  # PyMuPDF
from openai import OpenAI

from docling.datamodel.base_models import InputFormat
from docling.datamodel.pipeline_options import (
    PdfPipelineOptions,
    TableFormerMode,
)
from docling.document_converter import (
    DocumentConverter,
    PdfFormatOption,
)
from docling_core.types.doc import ImageRefMode


client = OpenAI()
VISION_MODEL = os.getenv("VISION_MODEL", "gpt-5.5")


# ---------- 1. Primary PDF extraction ----------

def extract_pdf(pdf_path):
    opts = PdfPipelineOptions(
        do_ocr=True,
        do_table_structure=True,
        generate_page_images=True,
        generate_picture_images=True,
        images_scale=2.0,
        queue_max_size=1,
    )
    opts.table_structure_options.mode = TableFormerMode.ACCURATE

    converter = DocumentConverter(
        format_options={
            InputFormat.PDF: PdfFormatOption(
                pipeline_options=opts
            )
        }
    )
    return converter.convert(str(pdf_path)).document


# ---------- 2. Deterministic QA ----------

# Illustrative critical-value detector; expand for FRU.
FACT_RE = re.compile(
    r"\b\d+(?:\.\d+)?\s*"
    r"(?:°[FC]|psi|minutes?|seconds?|hours?)\b",
    re.I,
)

def qa_page(pdf_page, markdown):
    issues = []
    original_text = pdf_page.get_text("text", sort=True)

    if not markdown.strip():
        issues.append("Empty extracted page")

    if "\ufffd" in markdown or "(cid:" in markdown:
        issues.append("Potentially corrupted characters")

    # Compare original PDF text when a usable text layer exists.
    if len(original_text.strip()) > 100:
        if len(markdown.strip()) < len(original_text.strip()) * 0.4:
            issues.append("Possible substantial text loss")

        expected = set(FACT_RE.findall(original_text))
        actual = set(FACT_RE.findall(markdown))
        if expected - actual:
            issues.append(
                f"Possible missing critical values: "
                f"{sorted(expected - actual)}"
            )

    # A scanned page has no reliable original text layer.
    if len(original_text.strip()) < 30:
        issues.append("Scanned/empty/corrupt PDF text layer")

    return issues


# ---------- 3. Vision LLM audit ----------

def audit_with_vision(pdf_page, markdown, page_no):
    pix = pdf_page.get_pixmap(
        matrix=fitz.Matrix(2.5, 2.5),
        alpha=False,
    )
    encoded = base64.b64encode(
        pix.tobytes("png")
    ).decode("ascii")

    prompt = f"""
You are checking a faithful PDF-to-Markdown extraction.

PDF page: {page_no}

Compare the ORIGINAL page image to the extracted Markdown.

Check:
1. Missing paragraphs, headings and warnings
2. Wrong or missing numbers, units, symbols and model IDs
3. Missing/reordered procedure steps
4. Incorrect table cells, columns or reading order
5. Missing figure labels or essential diagram content

Do NOT summarize or infer missing facts.

Reply with:
PASS - if no material discrepancy is visible

or:
REVIEW - followed by a precise list of discrepancies,
including proposed corrections where clearly legible.

Explicitly mark anything unreadable or uncertain.

EXTRACTED MARKDOWN:
{markdown}
"""

    response = client.responses.create(
        model=VISION_MODEL,
        input=[{
            "role": "user",
            "content": [
                {"type": "input_text", "text": prompt},
                {
                    "type": "input_image",
                    "image_url": (
                        f"data:image/png;base64,{encoded}"
                    ),
                    "detail": "high",
                },
            ],
        }],
    )
    return response.output_text


# ---------- 4. Assemble document and QA report ----------

def convert_manual(pdf_path, out_dir, audit_all=False):
    pdf_path = Path(pdf_path)
    out = Path(out_dir)
    out.mkdir(parents=True, exist_ok=True)
    (out / "assets").mkdir(exist_ok=True)

    document = extract_pdf(pdf_path)
    pdf = fitz.open(str(pdf_path))

    pages = []
    report = []

    for i, page in enumerate(pdf, start=1):
        md = document.export_to_markdown(
            page_no=i,
            image_mode=ImageRefMode.REFERENCED,
            image_dir=out / "assets",
            image_uri_prefix="assets/",
            traverse_pictures=True,
        )

        issues = qa_page(page, md)
        audit = None

        if audit_all or issues:
            audit = audit_with_vision(page, md, i)
            (out / f"page_{i:03d}_audit.txt").write_text(
                audit, encoding="utf-8"
            )

        pages.append(f"\n\n<!-- PDF_PAGE:{i} -->\n\n{md}")

        report.append({
            "page": i,
            "deterministic_issues": issues,
            "vision_audited": audit is not None,
            "vision_result": audit,
        })

    sha256 = hashlib.sha256(pdf_path.read_bytes()).hexdigest()

    header = (
        f"# {pdf_path.stem}\n\n"
        f"Source PDF: {pdf_path.name}\n\n"
        f"SHA256: {sha256}\n\n"
        f"PDF pages: {len(pdf)}\n"
    )

    # Draft only: audits must be resolved before promotion.
    (out / "draft.md").write_text(
        header + "".join(pages),
        encoding="utf-8",
    )

    document.save_as_json(
        out / "document.json",
        image_mode=ImageRefMode.REFERENCED,
    )

    (out / "qa_report.json").write_text(
        json.dumps(report, indent=2),
        encoding="utf-8",
    )

    pdf.close()
    return report


if __name__ == "__main__":

**First: Preserve figures as actual evidence.**

**Second: Make QA risk-sensitive.**

A typo in a generic introductory paragraph and an incorrect temperature or voltage specification should not receive equal review priority.

## 1. Your objection is correct in one important sense

**A later “checker” is not magic.**

If the later tool truly knew the content better in every way, then yes — often we should just use that later tool as the main extractor.

But in practice, **extractor** and **checker** do different jobs.

| Role | Main job | What it is optimized for |
|---|---|---|
| Extractor | Produce the full document output | Speed, structure, cost |
| Checker | Detect likely mistakes | Suspicion, comparison, QA |

## 2. Why a checker can still help without being “better at extraction”

A checker does **not** need to be globally better at extraction.

It only needs to be better at one narrower task:

- spotting missing values
- spotting weird characters
- spotting table mismatch
- spotting order problems
- spotting disagreement with another representation

- **Docling** may extract 95 pages fast and well.
- **Vision LLM** may be too expensive/slower to extract all 95 pages well every time.
- But the Vision LLM may still be useful for checking **page 47 troubleshooting table** after Docling flags that page as suspicious.

## 4. The real non-magic way to detect “problematic extraction”

There are only 4 practical sources of suspicion:

| Signal | Example |
|---|---|

| Cross-method disagreement | Docling says 25–120 psi, another method differs |
| Source-vs-output mismatch | PDF page visibly has a table, MD does not |
| Gold-fact checks | known key facts are missing |

That means the “check” is really:

> **compare outputs, or compare output to source, using limited rules**

—not magical truth detection.

### Two COS response modes

| Mode | What management receives | Appropriate use |
|---|---|---|
| **Quick insight** | Wiki-based synthesis, marked provisional | Brainstorming, orientation, identifying possible relationships |
| **Evidence-backed research** | Retrieved and checked information, citations, explicit unresolved questions | Decisions, financial claims, product specifications, repair or warranty advice |

The COS can use the Wiki to guide its searches. But **important factual claims should be verified against original records by default**, rather than assuming a warning makes an incorrect answer acceptable.

**Yes. I included a limited form of Agentic RAG in A2 ("one targeted expansion"), but I failed to make it explicit in both A2 and A3.** It should be a distinct capability in our target architecture.

The key is that **reranking and agentic retrieval do different jobs**:
